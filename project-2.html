TiltTwin

The brief was to share expertise between two people through physical movement. Our expert teaches a beginner how a 3D part rotates. The beginner doesn't drag a mouse: they hold a phone and turn it, and the part on the screen turns with it.

01 · The brief

Teach with your hands.

One person has a skill and another needs it, and they aren't in the same room. The brief asked us to move that expertise across through embodied input and output, so the body does part of the communicating. A video call or a shared screen couldn't be the core of the idea.

We picked 3D rotation in CAD. Beginners in Blender or Fusion struggle with it because the mouse mapping is abstract: which axis is this drag turning, and which way? An expert can't easily say it in words either.

The setup

One expert on a laptop, one beginner on a phone, remote and live, joined by a 4-letter room code.

02 · Diverge

Ten ways to move knowledge.

Each sketch answers one question: how can an expert's sense of 3D orientation reach a novice through the body instead of through words or a shared screen? We used the in-class ten-ideas method. Kevin drew the sketches and Ayushi and I gave feedback on them.

03 · Converge

The pivot from CADSense.

Our first direction, CADSense, was a mouse-driven tutor for extrude, rotate and scale. When we checked it against the brief, it looked conventional. A quarter of the grade is for creative embodied input and output, and mouse input is neither.

We scored the strongest concepts against five criteria taken straight from the brief.

#3 Object tracing2113310
#4 Mirror hand motion322119
#5 Rotate a physical proxy3231110

#2 + #7 + #10 → TiltTwin3332213

Columns: embodied input, embodied output, conveys CAD skill, feasible in 2 weeks, reliable live demo. Scores run from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong). They are our own design judgments, not measured results.

The physical-proxy idea (#5) scored high on embodiment, but it needs tracking hardware or computer vision, and we couldn't make that reliable in two weeks. Then we noticed that a phone already has orientation sensors. The phone itself could be the proxy.

Decision

Merge #2 (turn something physical), #7 (rotate until it's right) and #10 (the expert sets the lesson) into TiltTwin. We gave up extrude and scale so we could make one interaction, rotation, work well. That also leaves a clean comparison with mouse rotation for later.

04 · Refine

Keep four ideas, drop one.

We sketched five refinements: (1) basic physical rotation, (2) a transparent ghost of the target over the part, (3) axes and direction arrows that rotate with the part, (4) a check mark when the orientation is right, and (5) both people's actions kept in sync. We kept all but number 2.

Why the ghost went

On a phone-sized screen, two overlapping see-through L-brackets are hard to read. You can't tell which edges belong to which part, and near the target the two shapes blur into one. It also invites matching shapes by eye, when we wanted the beginner to learn which axis and which direction. We replaced it with three cues, one per question:

Where should it end up?
→ A small window with the expert's demonstration, looping from 0° to the target.
Which way do I turn?
→ A coloured arc arrow around the lesson's axis, from the current angle to the target.
How far am I?
→ A large live gauge ("22° of 45°") and colour: orange when far, yellow when close, green when matched.

05 · Storyboard & wireframes

Drawing the whole exchange.

The storyboard follows one lesson: the expert sends an instruction, the beginner calibrates, turns the phone, and both screens show a check mark. Then two rounds of wireframes pinned down what each person sees.

The change between v1 and v2 mattered most. A live "30° / 45°" gives the expert something exact to correct ("15° more") and gives the beginner an exact goal. v2 also makes turning the phone the input, in place of on-screen controls.

06 · Build

Baseline first, then the real thing.

Kevin built v1, a mouse-drag baseline with an 8-way arrow pad, in a single browser tab. It taught us three things we turned into requirements for v2: free two-axis rotation is hard to put into words, feedback should be continuous instead of waiting for a "Check Angle" button, and the two roles need to be on two real devices.

I wrote the v2 requirements and built it with Claude: phone tilt with calibration, live sync over Firebase, and the Vercel deploy. These were the decisions that shaped it:

The phone is the part

Turning the phone turns the model the same way. The hand learns the rotation as well as the eye. We considered on-screen arrows (that was v1) and full three-axis matching, which is too hard to judge against one target.

One axis per lesson

Hands wobble. We measure only the turn around the lesson's axis (a swing-twist split of the phone's rotation), so the sideways wobble doesn't make the feedback jitter. Euler angles would have hit gimbal lock when the phone is upright.

Calibration

The pose you're holding when a lesson arrives becomes 0°. Everyone holds a phone differently, and the compass drifts indoors, so a relative zero beats "hold it exactly like this".

Hints in body terms

"Tilt the top toward you", not "X+". That turns CAD axis language into something a hand can do. (Testing later showed this is harder than it sounds. See the Y-axis hint below.)

Four feedback channels

Colour, number, arrow, and sound with vibration. A beginner's eyes are on the part, so redundant cues help, and a buzz confirms success without looking.

A drag fallback, on purpose

Phones and laptops without motion sensors can still do the lessons by dragging. It also gave us the mouse condition for a later comparison.

Rooms use a 4-letter code in Firebase Realtime Database, which syncs in under a second with no server of our own. The codes skip look-alike characters (no 0/O or 1/I). We rejected WebRTC because it fails behind some networks, which was too risky for a live demo. A match counts when the part stays within 8° of the target for 0.8 seconds, so passing through the target doesn't count as finishing.

07 · Make it fit

From matching an angle to making a part fit.

With rotation working, we asked what would make it more useful for real CAD work without weakening the embodied part. We weighed four additions:

IdeaWhyCost or riskDecision
Part libraryPractise on parts you'd actually modelLowBuilt
Assembly "fit" taskGives the angle a purpose: you orient a part so it mates with anotherMediumBuilt
Hot/cold vibrationFeel the answer without lookingiPhone Safari has no vibration APIBuilt, with sound as the fallback
Stars and streaksFun and motivatingLow, but little link to sharing expertiseDeferred

Each part has a hole, an outlet or jaws, and a solid mating part sits where that feature will be at the target. That keeps the earlier decision to drop the transparent overlay, and it turns an abstract angle into a physical goal. The success moment becomes "it clicks into place". A unit test checks that no feature sits on an axis line, because rotating about that axis would never visibly move it.

Hot/cold. While a lesson is active the phone ticks. The ticks speed up (from about 1 to 9 a second) and rise in pitch as the part gets closer, and stop when it fits. Android also vibrates on each tick. iPhone Safari can't vibrate, so we use the light haptic iOS 18 and later gives when a native switch control is toggled. That behaviour is undocumented, so sound is the dependable channel.

08 · Testing

What a real hand found.

I tested in three layers: unit tests on the sensor maths, a real iPhone, then a small pilot with three people.

Unit tests, then a real phone

Seven tests check the sensor sign on all three axes, with the phone held in portrait and in both landscape directions. They all passed. Then on October 20 I ran the deployed app with an iPhone as the beginner, and the Y-axis lesson failed.

  1. Observed

    The X and Z lessons went as planned (8.1 s and 4.2 s in the session log). On Y, I moved the phone the opposite way from the one the hint meant.

  2. Diagnosed

    I suspected the maths first. I simulated each hold, made each hint's motion and checked the sign. It was right in all three. The wording was the problem: "turn the phone to the left" can mean the phone turns left, or the screen turns to face left. The X and Z hints can only be read one way, which is why only Y failed.

  3. Changed

    The hint now names a physical edge: "Swing the right edge of the phone away from you" (and "toward you" for negative angles).

  4. Re-tested

    Same iPhone, same day. X, Y and Z all moved the expected way. A second session that day checked all four parts and the hot/cold sound.

The lesson

Describe an embodied motion by a landmark on the device, not by a direction word whose meaning depends on how you're looking at it.

On October 21, Kevin tested an iPad as the expert and a phone as the beginner, both in Safari. The two connected, the part moved the expected way and messages crossed. This shows the interaction works on the devices we tried. It doesn't show it works on every phone.

Pilot study (n = 3)

Three participants each joined their own session on their phone and did three rotation tasks by turning it. A team member played the expert. Tasks covered all four parts, all three axes, and 45° and 90° turns in both directions. The expert's session log records time automatically, from the moment the instruction arrives to the first time the part is held on target.

P1Elbow, Y +45°: 6.5 sGear, Y +45°: 1.8 sWrench, Z −45°: 4.0 s4.1 s
P2Bracket, X +90°: 5.1 sGear, X +90°: 2.7 sGear, Z +90°: 4.0 s3.9 s
P3Bracket, Y −90°: 3.1 sBracket, Z −90°: 3.7 sElbow, Z −90°: 3.4 s3.4 s

All 9 tasks completed. Mean 3.8 s (SD 1.4), median 3.7 s, range 1.8–6.5 s.

Everyone finished every task. That includes the Y lessons that had failed before the rewording. Mean times were close across axes (X 3.9 s, Y 3.8 s, Z 3.8 s), so no axis stood out as harder. P1 and P2 were slowest on their first task (6.5 s and 5.1 s) and averaged 3.1 s afterward. That might be a warm-up effect, but with three people we can't separate it from task difficulty.

The part kept moving after "Fits!"

In all three sessions the expert's live readout had drifted far from the target after the success message: −119° on a −45° task, −23° on a +90° task, −37° on a −90° task. The likely cause is that people relaxed or lowered the phone once they felt done, and the expert's mirror kept following. To the expert, that looks like an error sitting next to a check mark.

Change after the pilot

Once the part fits, it stays snapped to the target on both screens until the next instruction arrives. I checked it by fitting a part, then turning the phone 150° away. Both screens held at the target, and the next instruction released the part back to live tracking.

What this doesn't show

Three people, no mouse condition, and I didn't record wrong-direction starts or ratings. It shows beginners can complete the tasks in about 4 seconds on every axis. It doesn't show that tilt is faster, easier or better for learning than a mouse. We designed a within-subjects comparison to test that (drag against tilt, counterbalanced order, measuring time, wrong-direction starts, corrections and a 1–5 understanding rating), but we haven't run it.

10 · Reflection

What I'm taking forward.

Test with the real device, early.

The Y hint passed every unit test and still failed in my hand. Software that reads sensors can be right while the instruction is wrong. I'd put the real phone in a real hand on day one, not after the build.

Words are part of the interface.

"Turn left" means two things depending on whether you watch the phone or the screen. Naming an edge of the device fixed it. I now treat instruction text as something to test, not something to write once.

Watch what people do after they succeed.

Nobody had mentioned the drift after "Fits!" in our walkthroughs. It only showed up with real participants, and it led to a real fix.

Say what the evidence doesn't show.

Three people finishing in about 4 seconds proves tilt works. It doesn't prove it beats a mouse, and the report says so.

If I did it again, and what's missing

I'd run the drag-versus-tilt comparison and record wrong-direction starts and ratings in the pilot. The biggest gap against the brief is that only the beginner is embodied. The expert still picks a part, axis and angle on screen, and the demo they send is an animation, not their own movement. Next I'd let the expert open TiltTwin on their own phone and demonstrate by turning it. The same orientation code would capture it, so the beginner sees a real motion replayed.

Sources I used beyond lecture

  • W3C. DeviceOrientation Event Specification. The alpha, beta and gamma angles and their rotation order. I checked the unit-test signs against it.
  • three.js DeviceOrientationControls. The maths for turning sensor angles into a quaternion. Our deviceQuaternion function follows it.
  • Dobrowolski, P. (2015). Swing-twist decomposition in Clifford algebra. arXiv:1506.05481. The standard technique for isolating the turn around one axis.
  • WebKit's <input type="checkbox" switch> (Safari 17.4+), the technique behind the iOS haptic workaround. It's undocumented behaviour, so I treat it as a technique, not a reference.
  • Google. Firebase Realtime Database documentation.
  • Anthropic. (2026). Claude [AI assistant], used for implementation under my direction.
