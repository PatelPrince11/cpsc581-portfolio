A Family of Buttons

The brief: design one button per teammate, and make the buttons interact so they reveal our team's dynamics. We made bubbles that float, collide, merge into shared interests, and move according to each person's personality.

01 · The brief

Four buttons. One team.

The assignment set two constraints: exactly one button per team member, and the buttons had to show team dynamics, meaning similarities, differences and relationships, not just four profiles. Everything had to be brainstormed, built, polished and documented in one week while four people worked around other classes and jobs.

02 · Diverge

Five concepts, sketched in parallel.

Before committing to anything we explored several directions on paper. Each storyboard walks through the interaction step by step, so we could judge the experience and not just a single screen.

Four mini-websites

A hub page with four buttons, each leading to a personal mini-site styled around that member.

Rejected Five pages to build, and it separates the team when the brief asks us to connect it.

03 · The pattern

Every idea was four separate buttons.

Comparing the concepts side by side showed a problem none of them revealed alone. Puzzle pieces, mini-sites and mountain peaks look different, but they share the same interaction: pick one person and see information about that person. None of them let the buttons interact with each other, which is where team dynamics would appear.

The visuals were different. The interaction was the same every time.

We turned that insight into explicit design criteria. We needed an interaction where:

  • exactly four primary buttons represent the four members;
  • each button says something about its member;
  • the buttons can directly interact with one another;
  • similarities and differences emerge from those interactions.

04 · Converge

Choosing with criteria, not taste.

We scored every concept against three questions. This meant giving up some of our most visually creative ideas. That was hard, but it was the right call.

05 · Refine

From four bubbles to a family.

The first bubble concept still described individuals. After Lecture 6 we asked what should happen when two bubbles touch, and that question gave the concept its point.

  1. Individual bubble
  2. →
  3. Their interests
  4. →
  5. Merged bubbles
  6. →
  7. Shared interests
  8. →
  9. Personality match
Adding a second layer of data

Hobbies are easy to grasp but shallow. We added results from our ITP personality survey, so a merge now shows what members enjoy together and also how alike they are across 30 personality traits.

06 · Personality in motion

Each bubble moves like its owner.

At first all four bubbles used the same random drift. Colour and icon told them apart, but their behaviour said nothing about the people. So we used the same movement algorithm with different parameters for each member, chosen from personality traits that translate naturally into motion:

Activity Level
→ Speed. Minimum and maximum travel speed. Mark's High Activity result makes his bubble the fastest by far (0.40–0.55 px/frame against Prince's calm 0.10–0.16).
Excitement-Seeking
→ Wandering. How often a bubble changes direction and how sharply it turns. Prince and Mark change course often, while Ayushi and Adrian hold a steadier line.

A deliberate limit

We considered mapping Gregariousness to attraction between bubbles, which was one of our clearest differences. But it would have needed real physics and a shakier psychological interpretation. All four of us also scored “Moderate” on Excitement-Seeking, so we tuned those values until the differences were perceptible, and we say plainly that they're not a literal conversion of the scores.

07 · Build

Prototyping by conversation.

We built the app with Claude Code, giving it the team's brainstorm document as the starting point and then refining step by step with prompts and manual edits. Our prompts were mostly design decisions. These are the turning points:

  1. “They feel too static… can I actually drag them together and have them merge?”

    Direct manipulation first. Real drift and real drag-to-merge, instead of a static grid of icons.

  2. “It should only merge if they actually have something in common… maybe have it bounce back.”

    Feedback for invalid actions. A rejected merge bounces back, so the user learns that some people don't share that interest.

  3. “Instead of expanding the bubble… have a card underneath that changes when I merge people.”

    A stable detail area. The canvas stays playful and a fixed card holds the dense personality comparison.

  4. “Don't make it only work for those 3… I might add Ayushi's report later.”

    Designing for change. Merging works for any group size and is data-driven, so new data needs no redesign.

  5. “Tab to one and use enter or space… if someone has reduced motion enabled…”

    Accessibility as a requirement. Keyboard support, a visible focus state, reduced motion and responsive bubble sizes.

  6. “Actually test everything… if you find bugs just fix them.”

    Verification. Automated Playwright runs covered dragging, merging, splitting, invalid merges, the 3-person case, mobile and keyboard.

Other polish: each member got a colour so the bubbles don't rely on icons alone, hovering shows the name and interest, emoji replaced custom icons (🏎️ 🚴 🌙 📚), and a soft pop sound confirms clicks, merges and splits.

08 · Data honesty

The interface never invents a connection.

The most important rule we set wasn't visual. A tool that generates code will happily generate plausible data too, so we made honesty an explicit requirement:

No shared hobby?

Show a neutral 🤝 handshake. Don't make up a match. (Adrian and Prince have no shared hobby, and the app says so.)

Missing report?

While reports were still coming in, we required the app to say “not enough data yet” and never estimate a match.

The percentage

Computed only from the 30 ITP traits. A trait matches only when every merged member shares the same Low/Moderate/High level. Mark + Prince show 73%. Add Adrian and it falls to 37%, and the app shows that.

We also re-checked every trait against the original reports after finding a transcription error, because a wrong input would have made the percentage wrong.

09 · My contribution

What I did.

  • Led the AI-assisted build. I wrote and iterated the prompt sequence above. I turned the team's brainstorm into working behaviour one decision at a time and reviewed each result before the next step.
  • Set the data-honesty rules. Merges only when a real shared interest exists, “not enough data” instead of guesses, and a match percentage computed from all 30 traits.
  • Pushed for general solutions. I made sure merging scaled from pairs to any group size, so teammates' data could be added without rework.
  • Accessibility and QA. Keyboard interaction, focus states, reduced motion, mobile sizing, and an automated test pass across every interaction state.

10 · Reflection

What I'm taking forward.

Divergence works, but only if you compare.

No single sketch showed us the problem. We only saw that every concept was four separate buttons when we laid them side by side. This matches Dow et al.'s finding that designers who prototype in parallel produce more divergent and better-rated work than those who iterate on one idea. Next time I'll write the design criteria before we sketch, so we can test sketches against them sooner.

Motion carries meaning.

Heider and Simmel showed that people read intentions and personality into simple moving shapes. Our bubbles relied on that: speed and wandering made a bubble feel like a person, without any extra text. I now see motion as part of the content, not decoration.

Directness is the point.

Dragging two people together to see what they share is direct manipulation in Shneiderman's sense: a visible object, a physical action and immediate feedback. It explained the idea better than any menu could. A rejected merge that bounces back taught the rules without an error message.

AI changes what a prototype answers.

Houde and Hill ask what a prototype is meant to test: role, look and feel, or implementation. AI-assisted building let us test all three in a week. The trade-off is that the AI can produce believable but false content, so being honest about the data became a design responsibility.

If I did it again

I'd run a quick hallway test with people outside the team. We tested functionality thoroughly but never checked whether a first-time visitor could tell that bubbles can merge without the tooltips.
