A Family of Buttons

The brief asked for one button per teammate, with the buttons reacting to each other to show how our team works. We made bubbles that float, bump into each other, merge to show shared interests, and move according to each person's personality results.

01 · The brief

Four buttons. One team.

The assignment had two constraints. There had to be exactly one button per team member, and together the buttons had to show the team's dynamics: what we share, how we differ and how we relate. We had one week to brainstorm, build, polish and document it, around four people's other classes and jobs.

02 · Diverge

Five concepts, sketched in parallel.

Before committing to anything, we sketched several directions on paper. Each storyboard walks through the interaction step by step, so we could judge it from start to finish.

Four mini-websites

A hub page with four buttons, each leading to a personal mini-site styled around that member.

Rejected Five pages to build, and it separates the team when the brief asks us to connect it.

03 · The pattern

Every idea was four separate buttons.

Putting the concepts side by side showed a problem we hadn't seen in any one of them. Puzzle pieces, mini-sites and mountain peaks look different, but the interaction is the same: pick one person and read about that person. None of them let the buttons act on each other, and that's where team dynamics would show up.

The visuals were different. The interaction was the same every time.

So we wrote down what the interaction needed:

  • four primary buttons, one per member;
  • each button says something about its member;
  • the buttons can act on each other directly;
  • similarities and differences come out of those interactions.

05 · Refine

From four bubbles to a family.

The first bubble concept still described individuals. After Lecture 6 we asked what should happen when two bubbles touch, and the answer turned it into a concept about relationships.

  7. Shared interests

Adding a second layer of data

Hobbies are easy to read but don't say much. We added our ITP personality survey results, so a merge shows what members enjoy together and how alike they are across 30 personality traits.

06 · Personality in motion

Each bubble moves like its owner.

At first all four bubbles drifted the same way. Colour and icon told them apart, but the motion said nothing about the people. So we kept one movement algorithm and gave each member their own settings, based on two personality traits that map well onto motion:

Activity Level
→ Speed. Minimum and maximum travel speed. Mark's High Activity result makes his bubble by far the fastest (0.40–0.55 px per frame, against Prince's 0.10–0.16).
Excitement-Seeking
→ Wandering. How often a bubble changes direction and how sharply it turns. Prince and Mark change course often. Ayushi and Adrian hold a steadier line.

A deliberate limit

We thought about mapping Gregariousness to how strongly bubbles pull on each other, since it was one of our clearest differences. That would have needed real physics and a shakier reading of the data. All four of us also scored Moderate on Excitement-Seeking, so we tuned those values until the differences were visible. They aren't a literal conversion of the scores, and we say so.

07 · Build

Prototyping by conversation.

We built the app with Claude Code. We gave it the team's brainstorm document as a starting spec, then refined the result one prompt at a time, with some edits by hand. Most of our prompts were design decisions. These were the turning points:

  1. "They feel too static... can I actually drag them together and have them merge?"

    This got us real drift and drag-to-merge.

  2. "It should only merge if they actually have something in common... maybe have it bounce back."

    A rejected merge bounces back, which tells the user those two people don't share that interest.

  3. "Instead of expanding the bubble... have a card underneath that changes when I merge people."

    The canvas stays playful, and a fixed card below it holds the detailed personality comparison.

  4. "Don't make it only work for those 3... I might add Ayushi's report later."

    Merging works for any group size and reads from the data, so adding a teammate's report needed no redesign.

  5. "Tab to one and use enter or space... if someone has reduced motion enabled..."

    This added keyboard support, a visible focus state, reduced motion and bubble sizes that scale with the screen.

  6. "Actually test everything... if you find bugs just fix them."

    Automated Playwright runs covered dragging, merging, splitting, invalid merges, the three-person case, mobile and keyboard.

Smaller changes: each member got a colour so the bubbles don't rely on icons alone, hovering shows the name and current interest, emoji replaced our custom icons (🏎️ 🚴 🌙 📚), and a short pop sound plays on clicks, merges and splits.

08 · Data honesty

The interface never invents a connection.

Our most important rule was about the data. A tool that writes code will just as easily write believable fake data, so we made honesty a requirement:

No shared hobby?

Show a neutral 🤝 handshake and don't make up a match. Adrian and Prince have no shared hobby, and the app says so.

Missing report?

While reports were still coming in, the app had to say "not enough data yet" and never estimate a match.

The percentage

It comes only from the 30 ITP traits. A trait counts as a match when every merged member has the same Low, Moderate or High level. Mark and Prince show 73%. Add Adrian and it drops to 37%, and the app shows 37%.

After we found one trait entered wrong, we re-checked every trait against the original reports. A wrong input would have made the percentage wrong.

09 · My contribution

What I did.

I led the AI-assisted build and turned our team's ideas into a working app. I planned and revised the prompt sequence, split the work into small steps, steered each feature as it was built, and checked every result against the design and behaviour we wanted.

I made the main design and technical decisions: how the interface shows shared interests, how bubbles merge, how the match percentage is calculated and how missing data is handled. I also set the rules the app follows. Bubbles merge only when a real shared interest exists, missing data is reported instead of guessed, and the match percentage uses all 30 traits.

I wanted the core behaviour to cope with change. I pushed for merging to work with any number of people, so more teammates' data could be added without redesigning anything, and I adjusted the interaction logic so a merge behaves the same way whether it involves two people or four.

I was responsible for accessibility, responsiveness and testing. That covered keyboard navigation, visible focus states, reduced-motion settings and sizing on phones. I ran automated tests across the app's interaction states, went through the results and fixed the problems they turned up before we finished.

AI sped up the coding. The requirements, design direction, technical decisions and final checks stayed with me.

10 · Reflection

What I'm taking forward.

Going wide only helps if you compare.

No single sketch showed us the problem. We only saw that every concept was four separate buttons once we put them side by side. Dow et al. (2010) found something similar: designers who prototyped several ideas in parallel produced more varied and better-rated work than designers who refined one. Next time I'll write the design criteria before we sketch, so we can check the sketches against them earlier.

Motion carries meaning.

Heider and Simmel (1944) showed that people read intentions and personality into simple moving shapes. Our bubbles relied on that. Speed and wandering made a bubble feel like a person without any extra text. I now treat motion as content, the same way I treat text or colour.

Dragging explains itself.

Dragging two people together to see what they share is direct manipulation as Shneiderman (1983) describes it: you act on a visible object and see the result right away. It explained the idea better than a menu would have. A rejected merge that bounced back taught the rule without an error message.

AI changes what a prototype answers.

Houde and Hill (1997) ask what a prototype is meant to test: its role, its look and feel, or its implementation. Building with AI let us test all three in one week. The catch is that AI easily produces believable but false content, so keeping the data honest became part of the design work.

If I did it again

I'd run a quick hallway test with people outside the team. We tested every function, but we never checked whether a first-time visitor could figure out, without the tooltips, that bubbles merge.
